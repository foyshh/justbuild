How to Choose Materials That Will Still Look Good 10 Years From Now

Just Build La Studio 7 min read

Most material decisions get made in a showroom, under showroom lighting, holding a sample the size of a coaster. It's the worst possible way to judge how something will actually live in your home for the next decade, and it's how most people do it anyway. A few shifts in how you evaluate materials can save you from a very expensive regret.

Test beyond the showroom light

Showrooms are lit to flatter. Your home isn't. A stone or timber sample that looks warm and rich under a showroom's directional spotlighting can read completely differently under your own north-facing window or a warm-white downlight at 7pm. Wherever possible, take a physical sample home and look at it at different times of day, under your actual lighting, before committing. It's a small extra step that prevents the single most common material regret.

Understand wear patterns before you commit

Not every surface earns its keep the same way. A high-gloss finish on a kitchen island will show every fingerprint and scratch within months; the same finish on a low-traffic feature wall might last years without complaint. Before choosing a finish, ask honestly how much daily contact it will actually see (hands, feet, cleaning products, direct sun) and match the durability of the material to that answer, not to how it looked in the catalogue.

The honesty test

Some materials age with character; others simply degrade. Solid timber develops a patina. Natural stone develops a soft matte from years of use. Brass darkens into something richer. Compare that to a laminate that chips at the edge or a high-gloss surface that scratches and never quite looks new again. These don't age. They just get worse. When budget allows, materials that are honest about what they are (real wood rather than a wood-look laminate, real stone rather than a printed surface) tend to reward that decade-long horizon far better than their cheaper look-alikes.

A material that ages well doesn't need to look new forever. It just needs to look intentional at every stage of getting older.

Sample at scale, not swatch

A tiny swatch cannot tell you how a pattern, grain, or veining will read across an entire floor or wall. Where the budget allows, request a larger sample panel, or better, ask to see the material used in a completed project of a similar scale, before finalising. What looks subtle on a small chip can become overwhelming, or the opposite, once it covers thirty square metres.

Getting this right the first time is far cheaper than replacing it in year three, which is exactly why material selection deserves as much attention as the layout itself.
