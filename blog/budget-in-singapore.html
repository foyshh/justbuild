How Much Should You Really Budget for an Interior Design Project in Singapore?

Just Build La Studio 6 min read

Every homeowner asks the budget question first, and almost every guide online answers it badly, with a single number that sounds authoritative and means nothing, because it depends entirely on the flat, the finishes, and how much of the work is hidden behind the walls. What actually helps is understanding what a renovation and design budget is made of, so you can build your own number instead of borrowing someone else's.

What "budget" actually has to cover

Most first-time renovators budget for the part they can see (furniture, paint colours, a nice sofa) and forget the part that quietly consumes the largest share of the number: the works behind the finishes. A realistic budget usually has four buckets: renovation works (hacking, electrical rewiring, plumbing, carpentry, tiling), design and project management fees, furniture and loose furnishings, and a contingency you hope not to touch but should always set aside. Skipping any one of these isn't saving money. It's just deferring the cost until it becomes a surprise mid-project.

Rough ranges, and why they're only a starting point

As a general guide for the Singapore market, a cosmetic refresh of an HDB flat (new paint, flooring, light fittings, minor carpentry) tends to land somewhere in the tens of thousands. A fuller renovation involving hacking, rewiring, and custom carpentry across a 4-room flat commonly runs well into six figures once fittings and furniture are included. Condominiums and landed properties scale up from there, partly because of size and partly because more of the works (waterproofing, higher ceiling heights, more complex M&E routing) carry a premium. Treat every number you read, including these, as a starting range to sense-check a quotation against, not a quote in itself. The only number that matters is the one built from your specific unit, your specific scope, and an actual site visit.

A budget isn't a ceiling you're trying to stay under. It's a set of decisions about where the money should visibly show up, and where it shouldn't.

The line items people forget

A contingency of roughly 10 to 15% on top of your renovation quotation isn't pessimism. It's standard practice, because older flats in particular tend to reveal surprises once hacking begins: uneven substrates, outdated wiring, plumbing that needs more than a patch. Beyond that, budget for the costs around the renovation itself: temporary accommodation if the flat is unlivable during works, storage for existing furniture, and the small run of permit and application fees that come with any structural change. None of these are large individually. Together, unaccounted for, they're the most common reason a project feels like it's "gone over budget" when really, it was simply never fully budgeted in the first place.

A better question than "how much"

The homeowners who end up happiest with their spend aren't the ones who found the cheapest quotation. They're the ones who were deliberate about where the money went. Structural and services work (waterproofing, electrical capacity, ventilation) is not the place to economise, because it's expensive and disruptive to redo later. Finishes and furniture, by contrast, can be phased: a considered shell now, with certain pieces added over the following year, is a perfectly reasonable way to manage cash flow without compromising the design intent. The right question isn't "what's the minimum I can spend?" It's "what does this money need to protect against having to be spent again?"

If you're at the stage of turning a rough number into an actual plan, that's exactly the conversation worth having before any quotation is drawn up, not after.
