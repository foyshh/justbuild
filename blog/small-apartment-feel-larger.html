How to Make a Small Singapore Apartment Feel Larger

Just Build La Studio 6 min read

In Singapore, square footage is currency, and most of us are working with less of it than we'd like. The good news: how large a space feels has surprisingly little to do with its actual floor area, and a lot to do with a handful of decisions that have nothing to do with knocking down walls.

Start with light, not layout

Before rearranging a single piece of furniture, look at how light moves through the flat across the day. Sheer curtains that let daylight diffuse rather than heavy drapes that block it, mirrors placed to reflect a window rather than a wall, and glass or open shelving in place of solid cabinetry near a light source. All of these do more for a room's perceived size than any amount of decluttering. A dim room reads small regardless of its actual dimensions; a bright one reads generous even when it isn't.

One flooring material, carried through

Changing flooring material at every threshold (tile in the kitchen, one timber tone in the living room, another in the bedroom) chops a small footprint into even smaller visual segments. Carrying a single flooring material and tone through connected spaces removes those visual stop signs, and the eye reads the whole area as one continuous volume instead of a series of small rooms.

Furniture scale, and letting the floor show

Bulky, boxy furniture that sits flush to the floor visually "fills" a room even when it's not physically large. Furniture on visible, slender legs lets the eye see floor beneath it, which reads as more open space than the furniture itself actually occupies. Multi-functional pieces (a bench that's also storage, a console that folds out) reduce the total furniture count without reducing what the room can do.

A small room isn't made larger by removing things from it. It's made larger by giving the eye fewer places to stop.

Storage that disappears

Built-in, floor-to-ceiling storage that matches the wall colour recedes visually in a way that a freestanding wardrobe or shelving unit never can, even at the same capacity. In small Singapore flats especially, the goal isn't less storage. It's storage that doesn't announce itself as furniture. Where possible, push storage to the room's edges and keep the centre open.

None of this requires a full renovation. Most of it can be tested with what you already own, before committing to anything permanent, which is exactly where a good design conversation should start.
